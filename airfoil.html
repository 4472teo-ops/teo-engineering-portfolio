02 / FIELD NOTES

Give an aerodynamic idea a shape.

The goal

Create an airfoil in CAD and understand how its geometry relates to the forces on a wing. I wanted to move from recognizing an airfoil shape to explaining why that shape matters.

The problem

An airfoil is not simply a curved outline. Its behavior depends on the geometry and the conditions in which it operates. A model can make the geometry visible, but it cannot by itself prove that the design performs well.

Airfoils are cross-sectional shapes used in wings and other lifting surfaces. Studying a section makes it easier to identify features such as the leading edge, trailing edge, curvature, thickness, and chord line. Those features give an engineer a precise way to discuss a design.

03 / FIELD NOTES

Four ideas behind the design.

01 / FORCE

Lift

Lift is the component of aerodynamic force perpendicular to the relative airflow. The pressure distribution around the airfoil and the turning of the airflow are connected descriptions of how that force develops. Both surfaces of the airfoil matter.

02 / RESISTANCE

Drag

Drag acts in the direction opposite the body’s motion relative to the air. Shape, surface effects, and operating conditions influence it. Producing lift can also carry a drag cost; more lift alone does not tell the whole performance story.

03 / FLOW

Airflow & pressure

The airfoil changes the flow around it, producing a pressure distribution over its surfaces. I learned to think about the whole flow field rather than assume that two particles separated at the leading edge must meet again at the trailing edge.

04 / ORIENTATION

Angle of attack

Angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and the relative airflow. Changing that angle changes aerodynamic behavior. Increasing it can increase lift over part of the operating range, but excessive angle can lead to flow separation and stall.

The design question is not “Which shape makes the most lift?” in isolation. Aerodynamic efficiency involves balancing lift and drag for a particular operating condition. A two-dimensional profile study is also different from analyzing a complete three-dimensional wing.

Technical background: NASA Glenn: What is Lift? and Factors That Affect Lift.

05 / FIELD NOTES

A convincing shape is not performance evidence.

The central challenge in this study was connecting a visible shape to aerodynamic behavior that I could not directly see in CAD. A smooth profile might look plausible, but appearance alone does not establish its lift, drag, or stall behavior.

I approached that gap by using research to explain the geometry. I could discuss the chord, surfaces, and orientation of an airfoil, then connect them to the aerodynamic concepts. That made the model useful as a learning tool without treating it as a validated aircraft component.

Another challenge was avoiding an overly simple explanation of lift. The upper surface, lower surface, pressure field, and flow direction are parts of the same physical interaction. Reducing the explanation to “air goes farther over the top” would miss that connection.

This case study discusses the design and reasoning challenges supported by the project. No failed wind-tunnel run, specific CAD failure, or measured performance improvement is documented.

06 / FIELD NOTES

Refining the explanation alongside the shape.

The study connected research to modeling and then back to explanation. Creating a model gave me a concrete reference for the aerodynamic ideas; reviewing those ideas helped me think more carefully about what the model could demonstrate.

There is no documented sequence of model dimensions or numbered revisions in the repository. I therefore cannot describe a particular thickness or camber adjustment as a tested improvement. The iteration I can explain is the connection between developing the design and improving my understanding of it.

For a future quantitative extension, I would choose a documented profile, define the chord and operating conditions, and compare versions while controlling the test conditions. That would turn a design study into a more rigorous performance comparison.

07 / FIELD NOTES

What was evaluated—and what wasn’t.

This was a CAD and concept study. Evaluation centered on completing the model and explaining how its shape relates to aerodynamic behavior. The available record does not include wind-tunnel measurements, CFD output, or flight tests of this airfoil.

Qualitative evaluation

  • A completed airfoil model
  • A connection between geometry and airflow
  • An explanation of lift and drag
  • An understanding of angle of attack

Evidence needed for performance claims

A defined test setup, operating conditions, and measured or simulated forces would be needed to compare aerodynamic efficiency. Those results are outside the completed project record.

That distinction matters. A successful CAD learning project is a useful outcome. It should not be presented as proof that the airfoil outperforms another design.

08 / FIELD NOTES

A completed design. A stronger explanation.

QUALITATIVE SUCCESS METRIC

Completed an airfoil CAD / design study.

I used the model to investigate and explain lift, drag, airflow, and angle of attack. Success was completing the design and connecting its geometry to those concepts.

The result is an educational design outcome, not a measured lift-to-drag ratio or a percentage increase in performance. The project gave me a better foundation for asking what data I would need to evaluate an airfoil more deeply.

09 / FIELD NOTES

Geometry needs context.

I learned that an aerodynamic shape cannot be judged separately from the conditions in which it operates. The angle to the airflow matters, and a change that helps one performance goal may create a tradeoff elsewhere.

CAD also helped me move between an abstract concept and a specific shape. It made me pay attention to the difference between drawing a component, explaining it, and testing it. Each step provides a different kind of evidence.

11 / FIELD NOTES

A closer look at the wing.

As a student pilot, I am learning how aircraft respond to control inputs and changing conditions. Studying an airfoil gives that experience another layer: the forces I feel and manage in an airplane begin with interactions between the aircraft and the air.

The project made angle of attack and aerodynamic tradeoffs easier to picture. It also made me more interested in the engineering choices that sit behind the aircraft I want to fly.

12 / FIELD NOTES

The model is a starting point for better questions.

I finished with a completed airfoil design study and a clearer understanding of the concepts behind it. The most useful next step would be a controlled comparison with a documented test method, so that a design decision could be supported by performance data.

For this project, the important progress was learning to connect shape, physical behavior, and evidence. That is a foundation I can bring into a more advanced aerospace project.
