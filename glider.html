01 / FIELD NOTES

My contribution

I was responsible for the glider from the initial build through testing and reflection. I worked individually, so the decisions—and the repairs after a bad launch—were mine to work through.

  • Designed and built the physical glider
  • Conducted flight tests and recorded time and distance
  • Observed unstable flights, crashes, and damage
  • Repaired and adjusted wings and stabilizing components
  • Adjusted nose mass and changed launch angle
  • Reduced the vertical stabilizer and added flaps
  • Retested after changes and compared the results
  • Reflected on what each design decision taught me
02 / FIELD NOTES

A simple goal. An unpredictable aircraft.

The goal

Build a glider that could travel farther and fly more consistently. I wanted a working aircraft I could test and improve, not just a shape that resembled one.

The problem

The early glider did not fly consistently. Some launches ended in crashes, and the crashes damaged the wings or stabilizing parts. A repaired aircraft also needed to be tested again: getting it back together did not guarantee it would behave the same way.

My early recorded distances were 29 and 30. Those flights gave me a starting point, but they did not explain the cause of every problem. Balance, launch technique, geometry, and the condition of the aircraft could all affect what I saw.

That made this a diagnosis problem as much as a building problem. Before trying to get a longer flight, I needed to understand which part of the design or launch I was actually changing.

03 / FIELD NOTES

Build → test → learn → repeat.

This timeline summarizes the engineering steps I worked through. Repairs and retests happened throughout the process; these are stages of the work, not a claim that every change happened once or that each stage maps to one recorded flight.

  1. Initial design

    I assembled the glider with its fuselage, wings, and stabilizing surfaces. The recorded fuselage length was 30.00 cm. The first version gave me something physical to evaluate.

  2. First test flights

    Early distances around 29–30 established a baseline. More importantly, the launches showed that the aircraft was not flying consistently.

  3. Crashes & breakage

    Some flights ended in crashes. Wings or stabilizing components broke or needed adjustment, sending me back to the build before I could make a useful comparison.

  4. Diagnose the problem

    I looked at how the aircraft behaved after each launch. I needed to separate damage, balance, launch angle, and geometry instead of treating every bad flight as the same problem.

  5. Adjust nose mass

    I changed the mass at the nose to explore how balance affected the glider. I then had to launch it again to see what that adjustment actually did.

  6. Change launch angle

    I adjusted the angle of the throw. Launching the same aircraft differently changes its starting conditions, so launch technique became part of the test process.

  7. Modify the vertical stabilizer

    I experimented with making the vertical stabilizer smaller. Removing material made the value of small, careful changes especially clear.

  8. Add flaps

    I added flaps and observed the effect on the aircraft. It was another opportunity to connect a change in the wing to a change in flight behavior.

  9. Retest

    After a change or a repair, I flew the glider again. The recorded results improved overall, but not every later flight went farther than the one before it.

  10. Final results

    The best recorded distance reached 63. That was 33 above the early result of 30, an improvement of 110%. The result mattered because of the process that led to it.

04 / FIELD NOTES

When a test ended in a repair.

The frustrating part was not just watching the glider hit the ground. It was picking it up and finding that a wing or stabilizing part had broken or shifted. Before I could test another idea, I had to get the aircraft back into a condition where it could fly.

A crash also made the next test harder to interpret. If the aircraft behaved differently after a repair, I could not automatically credit the last intentional modification. The repair itself might have changed the shape or alignment. I had to pay attention to the condition of the whole glider, not only the part I was trying to improve.

I also had to resist the temptation to change several things after a disappointing flight. Adjusting the nose mass, launch angle, and tail all at once might produce a different flight, but it would leave me guessing about why. Small, controlled adjustments gave me a better way to learn from each attempt.

The later record still includes setbacks: after a distance of 57, the next recorded values were 52 and 50. That is part of the story. The glider improved overall without becoming perfectly predictable, and a single good flight did not mean the work was finished.

The project record does not give an exact crash count, repair count, or a modification log for each numbered flight. I have kept those details unquantified.

05 / FIELD NOTES

Change one thing. Watch what changes.

My response was to rebuild when necessary, make a focused adjustment, and test again. The purpose of controlling variables was practical: I wanted a better reason for keeping or reversing a change than “that throw looked better.”

01 / BALANCE

Nose mass

Moving or adding mass at the nose changes the center of gravity. That affects how the aircraft balances and responds in pitch. I adjusted the nose mass and used subsequent flights to judge the behavior; I did not record a precise center-of-gravity location.

02 / INITIAL CONDITIONS

Launch angle

The launch sets the glider’s initial direction and energy. I changed the angle of the throw to explore a more useful transition into a glide. Launch angle is not the same as aerodynamic angle of attack, which depends on the direction of the relative airflow.

03 / GEOMETRY

Vertical stabilizer

The vertical tail contributes to directional stability and also adds surface area and drag. Making it smaller changed that tradeoff. I treated the cut as an experiment, not a rule that a smaller tail will always fly better.

04 / WING SHAPE

Flaps

Flaps change the local wing shape and can alter lift, drag, and pitching behavior. I added them to explore how the glider responded. The record does not isolate a distance gain caused by flaps alone.

05 / FEEDBACK

Repeated testing

A modification only became useful evidence after another flight. Retesting helped me compare outcomes, notice when a change did not help, and avoid judging the entire design by one launch. Repairs remained part of that loop.

This approach helped me become more deliberate, even though the project was not a laboratory-controlled experiment. Hand launches, repairs, and the absence of a change-by-change test log limit how precisely I can connect a particular modification to a particular result.

06 / FIELD NOTES

Small cuts have permanent consequences.

“You can always cut more,
but you can’t put it back.”MY TAKEAWAY FROM REDUCING THE VERTICAL STABILIZER

Changing the vertical stabilizer was different from adjusting the launch. I could try another launch angle immediately, but cutting material away changed the part itself. If I removed too much, another test would not put the original shape back.

That made gradual changes important. I learned to leave room for the next adjustment instead of trying to reach a final shape in one cut. It is a lesson I can use in any physical project where an operation is difficult to reverse.

The versions of the glider were connected by this pattern: build or repair, adjust, observe, and decide what to do next. I did not arrive at a perfect configuration all at once. I worked toward better flights while learning what each part of the aircraft was doing.

This is how I would improve the method next, not a claim about testing already completed.

09 / FIELD NOTES

The flight tells you something the drawing can’t.

I learned that small changes can have a large effect on an aircraft’s stability and flight performance. Balance, launch conditions, and surface geometry interact. A design that looks reasonable on a table still has to prove itself in the air.

I also learned to look at failure more carefully. A crash was disappointing, but it was useful when it led me to a specific question. The next adjustment needed a reason, and the next flight needed an observation—not just another throw.

Most of all, I learned the difference between getting a good result and understanding it. Reaching 63 was satisfying. Keeping the test record honest, including the shorter flights and the limits of what it proves, is part of becoming better at engineering.

11 / FIELD NOTES

Understanding the aircraft I want to fly.

As a student pilot, I am interested in more than operating an airplane. I want to understand why it behaves the way it does. Building and testing a small glider connected that interest to something I could hold, change, and watch.

The scale was small, but the habit was useful: notice a change, think about the cause, and respect the effect of a design decision. It made the connection between engineering and aviation much clearer for me.

12 / FIELD NOTES

The best result was learning how to improve.

If I only showed the final distance, I would leave out most of the project. The important work happened when the glider did not fly correctly, when a part broke, and when I had to decide what to try next.

I finished with a best recorded distance of 63 and a better understanding of how to work through a design problem. On my next build, I would keep a more detailed configuration log and test each version repeatedly. The process I want to carry forward is simple: make a thoughtful change, measure what happens, and keep learning.
