AEROSPACE · INDIVIDUAL DESIGN

Glider Design &
Flight Testing

I built this glider by myself, and the project became less about making a plane that looked right and more about figuring out why it kept failing, changing one thing at a time, and learning how small aerodynamic decisions can completely change a flight.

The problem

The goal was simple: build a lightweight glider that could launch consistently and travel as far as possible. The hard part was that a design that looks balanced on a table does not automatically fly well. My first attempts were unstable and I kept crashing, so I had to treat every failed launch as test data rather than just a failure.

My role

This was a solo project. I built the aircraft, made the design decisions, tested it, recorded results, and analyzed what changes were helping or hurting performance.

The process: build → test → break → improve

01 · Initial build

I built the glider with a roughly 30 cm fuselage, wings, a vertical stabilizer, and a horizontal stabilizer. At this point I was focused on getting the basic aircraft assembled and able to launch.

02 · The frustrating part

The early flights were inconsistent. I was getting distances around 29–30, and the glider was crashing repeatedly. This was the point where I realized that simply throwing it harder was not going to solve the problem.

03 · Changing the nose mass

I adjusted the mass toward the nose. This changed the aircraft's balance and helped it stop behaving as unpredictably. The lesson was that the center of mass mattered just as much as the physical shape of the wings.

04 · Changing launch angle

I also changed the angle at which I launched the glider. A launch angle that was too aggressive could cause the aircraft to climb and then lose energy or stall, while a flatter launch gave it a better chance to transition into a glide.

05 · Smaller aerodynamic changes

Once the major problems were under control, I made smaller changes. I reduced the size of the vertical stabilizer and added flaps. These changes were not random: I was trying to make the aircraft more controllable while preserving the distance it had gained.

06 · Test again

Each launch gave me another data point. My recorded distances progressed from 29 and 30 to 46, 50, 52, 55, 57, and finally 63. The improvement showed me that the iterations were actually changing the aircraft's performance.

The struggle was the most important part

“I learned to make small adjustments to your plane because you can always cut more, but you can't put it back.”

That was probably my biggest takeaway. The project taught me that engineering is rarely one perfect design followed by a perfect result. I had to accept that the glider would break, crash, or perform worse than expected, then use that information to decide what to change next.

Personal connection

I enjoyed this project because it connected directly to my interest in aviation. I am working toward becoming a pilot, so I already think about aircraft in terms of how they move through the air. Building something that I could physically throw, watch, adjust, and throw again made those ideas much more real than simply reading about aerodynamics.
